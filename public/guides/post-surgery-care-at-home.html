Post-surgery recovery at home

Recovering at home is usually more comfortable than staying in hospital — but it only works if the basics are in place. This is a practical checklist for the weeks after surgery.

Before discharge

  • Ask the hospital for written discharge instructions and a medication chart.
  • Confirm the follow-up appointment date and who to call in an emergency.
  • Get the prescription filled before the patient comes home.
  • Arrange the bed, a clear path to the bathroom, and a place to keep medicines and dressings.
  • Decide who will be present for the first few days and nights.

Wound care basics

Follow the dressing schedule the hospital gave you, and nothing more. More frequent changing is not better. Wash hands before and after, keep the area dry unless told otherwise, and never apply anything that was not prescribed.

Note the appearance of the wound each time and write it down — the date, what it looked like, and any smell or discharge. This log is what the doctor will want at follow-up.

Medication and pain

Use a written chart with times, not memory. Set alarms. Keep a separate list of what was taken and when, so doses are not doubled.

Some pain after surgery is expected. Pain that keeps rising, or that is not controlled by the prescribed medicine, is a reason to call the doctor rather than wait.

Mobility and preventing complications

Follow the movement advice you were given — usually gentle movement helps and prolonged stillness does not. Ask the doctor or physiotherapist how much walking is appropriate and whether any movements must be avoided.

Support the patient during the first walks. Remove loose rugs and cables. Keep a light on at night along the route to the bathroom.

Food, water and rest

Offer small amounts of fluids regularly. Follow any dietary instructions from the hospital, especially after abdominal surgery. Protein and fibre help recovery, but introduce food gently and stop anything that causes discomfort.

Rest is important, but so is not lying flat for too long. Change position regularly.

When to call the doctor immediately

  • Fever or chills.
  • Breathing difficulty or chest pain.
  • A wound that is opening, bleeding heavily, or has pus or a bad smell.
  • Pain that is getting worse instead of better.
  • Sudden swelling, redness or warmth in a leg.
  • Confusion, drowsiness or difficulty waking.
  • Not passing urine, or repeated vomiting.

Do not wait for the next appointment if any of these appear. This article is general information, not medical advice — always follow your treating doctor’s instructions.
