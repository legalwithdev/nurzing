How to choose a home nurse

Bringing a nurse into your home is a decision about trust. This guide walks through what to look for, what to ask, and how to set things up so care starts on the right foot.

First, know who you actually need

Home care usually involves one of three roles, and families often mix them up. A trained attendant helps with daily living — bathing, feeding, mobility, hygiene and companionship. A nurse handles clinical tasks such as medication, injections, wound care and monitoring. A doctor or hospital handles diagnosis and procedures.

Before you look for anyone, write down what the day actually needs. If the requirement is mostly daily-living support, an attendant is usually enough. If there are dressings, injections, catheters or monitoring, you need a nurse.

Questions to ask the agency

  • What exactly is checked before a professional is placed — identity, qualification, references?
  • Who is my point of contact if something goes wrong, and at what hours are they reachable?
  • What happens if the person assigned is not the right fit — is there a replacement process, and how fast?
  • What is the total cost, and what is included? Are there charges for replacement, night shifts or holidays?
  • Is the professional insured for accidents in the home?
  • Can I speak to or meet the professional before they start?

If an agency cannot answer these plainly, that itself is useful information.

Questions to ask the nurse directly

  • What is your qualification, and where did you train?
  • How many years have you handled cases like ours?
  • Which tasks are you comfortable with, and which will you not do?
  • How do you handle an emergency at night?
  • How do you keep family updated — a written log, a phone call, a message?

Documents worth checking

Ask to see identity proof and the qualification certificate. For attendants, ask what training they received and from where. If a police or background check is offered, ask what it covered and when it was done — a vague answer is not a check.

Keep a copy of the professional’s details, the agency contact, and your doctor’s number in one place at home.

The first few days

Treat the first days as a trial. Watch how the professional handles the patient, whether they follow the medication schedule, and whether they communicate clearly. Give feedback early — small corrections at the start prevent bigger problems later.

Set clear expectations on day one: timings, what is in scope, where things are kept, and how to reach you.

Red flags

  • Refusing to show identity or qualification documents.
  • Vague answers about what was verified.
  • No written agreement or receipt for payments.
  • Unwillingness to let family observe care.
  • Repeated lateness or unexplained absences without informing.

None of these are automatically disqualifying, but each one deserves a direct question.
