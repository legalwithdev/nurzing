Signs a parent may need support at home

Ageing is gradual, and the signs that someone needs help rarely appear all at once. This guide lists the changes worth noticing, and what to do about them.

Daily living

  • Uneaten food in the fridge, or very little cooking happening.
  • Unwashed clothes, or the same clothes worn repeatedly.
  • The house becoming noticeably untidy or unhygienic.
  • Unopened post, unpaid bills, or missed appointments.
  • Difficulty with buttons, cooking, or getting up from a chair.

Safety at home

  • Bruises or marks that are not explained.
  • New difficulty with balance or walking.
  • A fall, even one that did not cause injury.
  • Reluctance to leave the house or to bathe.
  • Medicines that are mixed up, duplicated or running out early.

Health

  • Noticeable weight loss or gain.
  • Increased confusion, or difficulty following a conversation.
  • New incontinence, or trouble reaching the bathroom in time.
  • More frequent visits to hospital, or repeated infections.
  • Complaints of dizziness, weakness or poor sleep.

Memory and mood

  • Repeating the same question within a short time.
  • Forgetting recent events while remembering the distant past clearly.
  • Getting lost on a familiar route.
  • Withdrawal from things they used to enjoy.
  • Irritability or low mood that lasts more than a few weeks.

Some of these are treatable — medication side-effects, thyroid problems, depression and vitamin deficiencies can all look like memory loss. A doctor should assess before anyone assumes dementia.

How to start the conversation

Lead with concern, not correction. Rather than listing faults, talk about what you have noticed and ask how they are finding things. Many people resist help because they fear losing independence, not because they disagree that they need it.

Frame help as support for specific tasks rather than a takeover of their life. Start small — help with medicines or a weekly visit — and build from there.

What to do next

If several of the signs above are present, arrange a medical assessment first. Then look at practical options: family rotation, an attendant for daily living, a nurse for clinical needs, or a combination.

Write down what the day actually requires before choosing anyone. It is easier to match help to a clear list than to a general feeling of worry.

This guide is general information for families planning care at home. It is not medical advice, and it does not replace an assessment by a qualified doctor. Always follow the instructions of your treating clinician, and in an emergency contact your local emergency number immediately.
